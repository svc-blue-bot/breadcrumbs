# My “SQ4R-ish” Study Loop for Real Depth (Not Just Exam Memory)

Most people study in a way that creates familiarity, not ability. You reread, rewatch, highlight, and it all feels “known”… but then you’re asked to explain it, apply it, investigate an issue, or make a decision under pressure and it falls apart.

This is my loosely adapted SQ4R-style method for building actual understanding: the kind that shows up in analysis, problem-solving, and real work (not just passing exams).

The key idea is simple:

The outcome isn’t the notes. The outcome is what your brain does while making them.

## Why memorization alone doesn’t take you far
Memorization can be useful for:
- terminology
- commands / flags
- definitions
- sequences

But deep competence requires being able to:
- explain a concept simply
- spot patterns and anomalies
- reason about trade-offs
- diagnose failures
- apply knowledge in new contexts

This method is designed to train those abilities, not just facts.

---

## The loop (my adapted SQ4R)
Classic SQ4R is Survey -> Question -> Read -> Reflect -> Recite -> Review.

My version maps cleanly to modern learning (videos, docs, labs, books) while keeping the important parts:
- Capture (raw notes)
- Condense (thinking notes)
- Create questions
- Recall (answer without looking)
- Review (mark gaps + fix)
- Revisit (spaced repetition + question rewrites)
- Repeat per topic or concept.

---

### Step 1: Capture notes
Right after or during learning a topic (video/chapter/article/lab), I dump raw notes:
- dot points
- definitions
- key terms
- commands / syntax
- paths / files
- workflows / sequences
- “gotchas” and exceptions

Rules for Capture:
- Don’t polish.
- You’re building a scratchpad, not a textbook.

---

### Step 2: Condense into “thinking notes” 
Now I re-read my raw notes and rewrite them as a short explanation using:
- Core Idea: the simplest accurate mental model
- Why it matters: where it shows up in real work + why you’d care
- Common mistake: the misconception / trap / failure mode

This is the part that actually trains you.

You’re practicing:
- choosing what matters (signal vs noise)
- compressing complexity into an explanation
- linking knowledge to consequences and use-cases

Important: the goal is not a comprehensive summary that covers everything. The goal is the internal reasoning you do while deciding what belongs.

---

### Step 3: Write ~6 questions that test use, not recognition
These questions are the bridge from “I read it” to “I can apply it.”

I usually write 6 with a mix like:
- Explain: Describe the concept in 2–4 sentences like you’re teaching a junior.
- Mechanism: What happens step-by-step when it runs / occurs?
- Differentiate: How is this different from a similar concept? When would each apply?
- Failure mode: If it breaks/misconfigures, what symptoms would you expect?
- Scenario: Given a short scenario, what’s your best hypothesis and why?
- Decision/trade-off: What choice would you make in a real situation and what’s the risk?
- Rule: avoid having all your questions that can be answered by copying a line from your notes. If a question feels “too easy,” it’s probably testing recognition, not understanding.

---

### Step 4: Wait ~10 minutes, then answer without looking
This delay is deliberate.

If you answer immediately, you’re still riding short-term memory and note-echoing. Waiting forces your brain to retrieve, reconstruct, and explain exactly what you need in real situations.

No peeking. Even “just to confirm a command” undermines the point.

---

### Step 5: Review your answers and write a short “miss log”
Compare your answers to your notes and mark:
- what was wrong
- what was missing
- what was vague

Then write a quick miss log like:
- Missed: (what you got wrong)
- Likely reason: (confusion, weak model, mixed two ideas, didn’t understand mechanism)
- Fix: (add a clarification line, or write a better question)

This turns mistakes into targeted improvement instead of repeating the same confusion forever.

---

### Step 6: Revisit with spacing
I answer the same questions again:
- in 1–2 days
- then another ~2 days later (if required due to the depth of topic or it’s simply not sticking)

If I’m still getting them wrong:
- I rewrite the questions so I can’t memorize the phrasing
- I create a new scenario
- I force a decision (choose X vs Y and justify)

That’s how you stop “remembering the answer” and start building transfer.

The lab/project variant (when hands-on learning is doing the heavy lifting)
- If you’re learning through a practical lab or project, you can shorten it:
- Capture: commands, pitfalls, what you observed
- Condense: Core Idea / Why it matters / Common mistake
- Questions: write 2–3 scenario questions only (optional)

Sometimes the lab already provides the retrieval and context, so the question step can be lighter or skipped entirely.

---

## The part people ruin: rushing and why it makes this useless
If you rush this process, you strip out the exact friction that creates learning.

Rushing usually looks like:
- turning Capture into a transcript
- turning Condense into “rewriting the transcript but shorter”
- writing shallow questions (“define X” six times)
- doing open-book answering
- skipping the delay + spacing

It feels productive because you produce a lot of text. But you’re not training selection, explanation, and retrieval which are the things you actually need to learn effectively and efficiently.

If you only have time to do it properly for fewer topics, do that. One deep cycle beats five shallow ones.

---

## Another trap: getting an LLM to write your notes/questions/condense for you
If you outsource the writing to an LLM, you can end up with beautiful notes and weak understanding.

Because the value isn’t the artifact, it’s the cognitive work you do while creating it:
- deciding what matters
- building your own explanation
- designing questions that expose real gaps
- struggling through recall

If the LLM does those steps, you’ve skipped the exercise.

The only good way to use AI here 
Use an LLM after you’ve done the work, like this:
- After your Condense: “Here’s my Core Idea / Why it matters / Common mistake. What’s unclear, misleading, or missing?”
- After your Questions: “Which of these questions can be gamed by memorizing? Rewrite them to force reasoning and scenarios.”
- After your Recall attempt: “Here are my wrong answers, what misconception does this suggest, and what one question would target it?”

That keeps the learning in your brain while still getting high-quality feedback.

## Closing mindset
The goal isn’t to collect information. It’s to build a mind that can explain, apply, and reason with information.

If you rush, you lose the depth. If you outsource to an LLM, you lose the exercise.

Do the reps. Put in the work. Be an effective learner. Become better in your field. That’s the whole point.
