# Preparation Of Thought

People talk about exams like OSCP, BTL2, HTB CPTS, and other intermediate and beyond practical certs as “practical knowledge builders”. And yeah, they are. You learn tools, techniques, how things work, how to apply them. But that’s not really what changes you.

The thing is, they don’t actually teach you the most important part.

They don’t sit you down and explain how to think when everything is unclear. Or how long to chase an IOC before you’re just burning time. Or when to abandon a line of thinking that feels promising but isn’t going anywhere or worse yet, doesn't change anything. But you have to learn those things to succeed. 

You’re dropped into ambiguity and expected to function. You start with the obvious, not because it’s elegant, but because you need something solid to stand on. You reiterate through IOCs. You switch approaches depending on what you’re seeing. You follow breadcrumbs, not knowing if they’ll lead anywhere, just trying to build a bigger picture. All while the clock is ticking.

I’ve seen people prepare and prepare for these exams, grind labs, collect checklists, only to fall short when the situation doesn’t match the checklist. The steps are there, but the reasoning behind them isn’t. And when that happens, emotion creeps in. Panic, frustration, tunnel vision. The investigation stops being methodical and turns into scrambling for answers, hoping something sticks, until you end up with a nice sounding narrative, that then gets a further "polish" by an LLM with no real reasoning behind it.

Over time, what’s really happening is that your investigative methodology is being built and rebuilt in real time. You’re learning how to create structure where none is given. How to adapt without panicking. How to keep moving forward when the path isn’t obvious and no one is going to tell you if you’re “doing it right”.

And that mindset transfers. Red team, blue team, it doesn’t matter. The exam isn’t just testing what you know or how well you can apply it. It’s testing whether you can operate with the knowledge gained under pressure, inside uncertainty, long enough to figure something out. They don’t necessarily teach that skill. But you must learn it. Otherwise, you don’t make it through.

A piece to leave with, if you were to leave with anything: you do not know what failed if you do not reflect. This is a time-old process that rarely gets followed today. You don’t need to wait to be part of some mystical, non-existent incident response team to do lessons learned. Lessons learned starts with you. Every day. Every lab. Every investigation. The next process. The next approach. The next methodology. The next prepared thought. While you continuously refine your own process, so that truth-seeking holds up under pressure, and in the unknown.

And of course, lessons learned fall apart if you’re basing decision quality on outcomes. I touch on this more in my other article, “Outcomes write the report. Decisions shape the craft.”
