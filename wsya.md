# Who's steering your analysis ?

One thing I keep circling back to when investigating an incident is how much of the work is really about managing yourself. Not just your process, not just the evidence, but your own bias, your own reactions, and how quietly those things can start steering the analysis if you’re not paying attention.

I think investigations have to be approached with multiple hypotheses running at once. Not because that sounds "disciplined", but because you are almost never working with complete information. 

Where this starts to go wrong is when a hypothesis begins to feel right. Have you ever sensed that things are finally clicking into place. That’s usually the moment where it can get dangerous, because once something starts feeling like a coherent story, it gets a lot harder to stay honest with it. We're human right, naturally you'll fill gaps with assumptions and smooth over inconsistencies. 

That’s how investigations get pulled off course and how possibilities get dismissed too early. Not always because there wasn’t enough data, but because certainty showed up too early, and it felt good enough to trust.

That gets worse when you accept that mistakes are not some edge case here. In any kind of analytical work, error is part of it. In incident response, or really any environment where information is incomplete and the situation is changing while you’re trying to understand it, those errors compound fast and early. If you’re not actively checking your own limitations, they don’t stay in the background. They, as well as your early unchallenged assumptions, and blindly trusted emotions, start steering the entire direction of the work.

---

There’s another part of this that I don’t think people talk about enough, which is just your own emotional reaction to what you’re seeing.

When something clicks, you feel relief. When things stop lining up, you feel pressure to force resolution. When a narrative starts forming, you feel more confident. I don’t think those reactions are meaningless, but I also don’t think they’re trustworthy. They usually tell you more about what you want to be true than what is actually true.

So when a hypothesis feels especially satisfying, I don’t think that should automatically reassure you. Honestly, it should probably make you slow down. That kind of feeling can be a warning sign. It can mean you’re getting ready to act on something before you’ve really stress-tested it.

The best responders, analysts, investigators, whatever word you want to use as this isn't isolated to cybersec, are not people who somehow eliminate that part of themselves. They just get better at noticing it, notice when they’re getting attached to an explanation, notice when something feels a little too clean. They keep other possibilities alive longer than feels comfortable. They let go (but never completely) of good-looking explanations when the evidence stops supporting them.

Bruce Lee described it simply: be like water. That mindset applies directly to investigations. You adapt constantly, reshaping your understanding as new evidence comes in, all while treating your emotions as signals, not facts.

---

My Final Thoughts: LLMs and AI accelerating everything, analysis, mistakes, decisions, even the attacks themselves, it’s getting easier to be confidently wrong, faster. At the same time, compromises are happening at record speed, which means you have less time to correct mistakes. So this kind of thinking matters more than ever.
